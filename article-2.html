The last letter-writers: who still sends mail for the sake of it

A small but stubborn group of people who write letters, not for lack of a phone, but because of it.

By Ahsan Tariq · 5 min read · Culture

Every one of them owns a phone. That's the detail worth sitting with. The people who still write and post physical letters aren't doing it because they lack an easier option — they're doing it because the easier option is exactly what they're avoiding.

A message typed on a phone arrives instantly and is answered, often, just as fast. A letter takes days to arrive and invites a reply with no particular deadline. That gap — the days in between — is not a flaw in the system. For the people who keep using it, the gap is the entire point.

Slowness as a feature

Handwriting forces a kind of editing that typing doesn't. You can't easily delete a sentence once it's on paper, so the sentence gets thought through before it's written, not after. Several letter-writers described this as the main appeal: fewer, more considered words, rather than more words sent faster.

There's also the object itself. A letter can be kept in a drawer, reread years later, handed to someone else entirely. A text thread can be scrolled back through, technically, but almost never is.

Not nostalgia, exactly

It would be easy to file this under nostalgia, but most of the people keeping the habit alive are not old enough to be nostalgic for a pre-digital childhood — many grew up entirely online. Their reasons are closer to a diet than a memory: a deliberate, occasional cutting-back of a fast channel, in favour of a slow one, for the sake of the slowness itself.

Whether the habit spreads or stays a niche is beside the point for most of them. As one described it, the letter isn't a protest against modern communication — it's just the one part of the week where nothing is expected to arrive immediately.
