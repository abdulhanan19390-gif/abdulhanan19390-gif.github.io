Why slower internet is making a quiet comeback in small towns

As fibre rollouts stall in low-density areas, a handful of towns are finding that older, steadier connections are enough to run a business — and easier to fix when they break.

By Hina Raza · 6 min read · Technology

In towns too small for fibre companies to prioritise, a strange thing has started happening: people have stopped waiting. Instead of holding out for gigabit speeds that keep getting pushed back a year, shopkeepers, clinics, and small offices are settling for the copper and fixed-wireless links they already have — and building around them instead of against them.

It isn't a triumphant story. Nobody prefers a slower connection. But the towns where this shift is most visible share a pattern: someone local learned to keep the existing setup reliable, and reliability turned out to matter more than raw speed for most day-to-day tasks.

What changed

A card payment doesn't need much bandwidth. Neither does a booking form, an inventory spreadsheet synced once an hour, or a video call at 480p. What breaks small businesses isn't a slow line — it's an unpredictable one, where the connection drops during a transaction and nobody nearby knows how to fix it.

Local technicians who understand the old wiring have become more valuable, in some cases, than the promise of a new one. A repair that used to mean a week's wait for a regional contractor now takes an afternoon, because the fix is understood locally rather than escalated.

"We didn't upgrade. We just stopped losing the connection every time it rained," said one shop owner, describing a fix that cost less than a new router.

The trade-off nobody advertises

This isn't an argument against better infrastructure — everyone interviewed for this piece would take faster, more resilient fibre if it arrived tomorrow. The point is narrower: while waiting, "good enough and understood" has quietly outperformed "fast but fragile" for the businesses that depend on it daily.

Whether that lesson survives once fibre does eventually reach these towns is an open question. For now, it's a reminder that the newest option isn't always the one that keeps the lights — or the card reader — on.
